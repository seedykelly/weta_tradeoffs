1 Results

1.1 Sample composition and body-size overlap

The analysis included 313 individuals: 118 females, 78 eighth-instar males, 79 ninth-instar males, and 38 tenth-instar males. Mean pronotum length was 7.81 ± 0.41 mm in females, 6.37 ± 0.37 mm in eighth-instar males, 6.93 ± 0.32 mm in ninth-instar males, and 7.52 ± 0.31 mm in tenth-instar males. All four groups overlapped in pronotum length from 6.99 to 7.49 mm. The reference size of 7.2 mm used for adjusted comparisons therefore lay within the region of common support.

1.2 Locomotory morphology

1.2.1 Relative allocation among leg pairs

The joint repeated-trait model showed that group differences depended strongly on leg identity (F(6, 622.86) = 100.35, p < 0.001). Thus, the male developmental groups were not uniformly longer- or shorter-legged than females; instead, the magnitude and direction of group differences varied among forelegs, midlegs, and hindlegs. There was also evidence that group-specific allometric slopes depended on leg identity (F(6, 622.72) = 2.18, p = 0.044), although this three-way effect was much weaker than the group-by-leg interaction. The body-size-by-group interaction was also supported within the joint model (F(3, 312.31) = 3.62, p = 0.013).

At a pronotum length of 7.2 mm, eighth-, ninth-, and tenth-instar males had forelegs that were 5.8% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), 11.4% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), and 16.9% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), respectively, than those of females. Midlegs were 2.1% longer (Holm-adjusted p = 0.057), 7.5% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), and 12.2% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), respectively. Hindlegs showed a contrasting pattern: eighth-instar males had hindlegs that were 6.3% shorter (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), ninth-instar males had hindlegs that were 0.5% shorter (Holm-adjusted p = 0.555), and tenth-instar males had hindlegs that were 3.9% longer (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001) relative to females. Overall, foreleg enlargement increased progressively across the male developmental groups, midleg enlargement was concentrated in the ninth- and tenth-instar groups, and hindleg morphology followed a different trajectory.

1.2.2 Allometric variation

Estimated allometric slopes were below one for all group-by-leg combinations, indicating negative allometry of leg length relative to pronotum length. Tenth-instar males had the shallowest estimated slope for all three leg pairs. After Holm correction within each leg, the clearest specific contrast was the hindleg slope difference between females and tenth-instar males (difference = 0.537, p = 0.003). The exhaustive decomposition of the three-way interaction further showed that the female–eighth-instar slope contrast differed between forelegs and hindlegs (difference-in-differences = -0.193, p = 0.048). Females and eighth-instar males therefore had nearly parallel foreleg allometries, whereas their hindleg allometries were more divergent. Other corrected slope contrasts were not clearly supported.

1.2.3 Femur and tibia components

Group-specific allometric slopes were supported for Forefemur, Midfemur, Hindfemur, Hindtibia; the remaining segment models were adequately described by common slopes. These secondary analyses indicate which femur or tibia components contributed to the group-level differences in total leg allometry.

1.3 Sensory morphology

1.3.1 Ear size and foretibia length

The ear–foretibia slope did not differ among groups (F(3, 301) = 1.33, p = 0.264). Across groups, linearized ear size increased with foretibia length with an allometric slope of 0.795 (95% CI 0.673–0.917). The ear–pronotum interaction was also not supported (F(3, 304) = 0.86, p = 0.464). After accounting for pronotum size and foretibia length, Eighth-instar males were 5.0% smaller in area than females (Holm-adjusted p = 0.013); Ninth-instar males were 5.7% smaller in area than females (Holm-adjusted p = 0.005); Tenth-instar males were 5.7% smaller in area than females (Holm-adjusted p = 0.012).

1.3.2 Eye length and head size

The eye–head-size slope did not differ among groups (F(3, 301) = 1.97, p = 0.118). Across groups, eye length scaled with head size with an allometric slope of 0.575 (95% CI 0.469–0.682). The eye–pronotum interaction was not supported (F(3, 304) = 1.03, p = 0.380). At a pronotum length of 7.2 mm and mean head size, Eighth-instar males were 10.8% shorter than females (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001); Ninth-instar males were 13.4% shorter than females (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001); Tenth-instar males were 12.5% shorter than females (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001).

1.4 Model diagnostics

Observed and model-predicted trait distributions corresponded closely. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and showed broadly homogeneous variance, with only minor curvature near the extremes of the fitted ranges. Collinearity remained below conventional concern thresholds, and no observation showed sufficient influence to justify exclusion after the underlying measurements had been checked. The log–log linear models and the repeated-trait mixed model were therefore retained for inference.
